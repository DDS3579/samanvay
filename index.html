The project in plain words

No technical background needed. Samanvay is a farm manager that never sleeps. It checks how dry each farm is, decides who needs water most, waters them, and tells you why. Here is the idea in four pictures.

The sensor notices

The brain thinks

3. Why a smart brain, not a timer

Say the tank holds 50 litres, but the farms want 80. A timer would give everyone the same share and some crops would still wilt. Samanvay shares it by who needs it most.

The problem

Many farms draw from one river, lake or tank. When the source runs low, several farms go dry at the same time and someone has to decide who gets water first. Today that decision is usually a fixed rotation or a guess. Neither accounts for how dry each farm actually is, how much water is left, or whether rain is coming. And water is only one of many decisions: what to plant, what the soil needs, when heat becomes a threat.

Wasted water

Timers water farms that don’t need it while another farm wilts.

No live data

Nobody knows the real soil moisture or the real source level at the moment of the decision.

No single view

Water, weather, soil and crop decisions live in different places, or only in the farmer’s head.

What we build

Open one app and see the whole farm: what is dry, what is too hot, what the soil likely needs, and what to do next. Behind it, a team of AI agents watches the sensors and runs the farm. Water comes first: four farms share one reservoir, and the agents decide who gets water, how much and when, then explain every decision.

The core engine never mentions farming. It only knows four things, so the same engine can later run other domains.

Resource

A limited pool with a level and a refill or drain rate. Here: the reservoir.

Consumers

Entities that request from the pool, each with a need and a priority. Here: the farms.

Actuators

How an allocation physically happens. Here: pumps and valves.

Policy

The rules and goals: survival thresholds, fairness, water saved.

The agent team

Each agent’s numbers come from small prediction models and rules. The language model only explains and never decides.

Irrigation agent

Decides water for each farm from moisture, drying speed and reservoir level. Acts on its own, inside safety limits.

Climate agent

Watches air and soil temperature and humidity. Flags heat stress, cold nights and good planting windows.

Crop planner

Suggests what to plant next from season, temperature, soil moisture and local conditions.

Soil advisor

Estimates likely fertilizer or lime needs from crop stage, watering history, weather and optional manual soil-test entries. Advice only.

Arbiter

Settles conflicts between agents and shares limited resources like water. Final say, inside safety limits.

Acts on its own: pump and valve control. Waits for your approval: fertilizer, planting and any other advice. Why no NPK sensor: cheap NPK probes are unreliable, so nutrient advice comes from crop-stage rules, farm history and optional soil-test entries (pH strips or a lab report), and is labeled as an estimate.

In scope: core engine, agent team, mobile-friendly app, sensor simulator, benchmark against a plain timer. Out of scope for now: NPK sensors, pest or disease detection, LoRa, solar, cloud hosting, user accounts, a native mobile app, real-farm deployment. Satellite is a bonus at the end of this page.

How a decision gets made

The loop runs every few seconds. The allocator makes the decision; the agents explain and negotiate it in words.

Sense

Each node sends soil moisture, soil and air temperature, and humidity. The reservoir node sends level, rain and light.

Clean

Smooth the noisy readings, calibrate them, and flag any sensor that goes silent or jumps.

Predict

Small ML models (such as regression or gradient boosting) estimate each farm’s drying rate, time until critical, and how fast the reservoir is falling. Rain forecast adjusts the estimate.

Score need

Each farm gets an urgency score from its moisture gap, drying speed, crop sensitivity and time since last watering.

Negotiate

Irrigation, climate, crop and soil agents state their case. The arbiter weighs them against the water budget left in the reservoir. The numbers come from the models, not the language model.

Allocate

The deterministic arbiter splits the available water: who gets it, how much, in what order. In a shortage it protects farms closest to crop loss first.

Check safety

Never run the pump dry, never overwater, never open two valves past the pump’s capacity. Any breach blocks the command.

Act

Water actions run automatically inside safety limits. Fertilizer and planting advice goes to the app and waits for your approval.

Explain

The language model turns the allocator’s numbers into plain words on the dashboard: “Farm B is 6 hours from critical, reservoir at 38%, so B gets 20 s now and A waits.”

Learn and repeat

Compare predicted and actual drying, update the model, and return to step 1.

Optional: water flow sensor

YF-S201 flow meter, about NPR 500. Lets the system measure water actually delivered to each farm.

Optional: soil test kit

pH strips or a basic test kit, a few hundred NPR. You type the result into the app and the soil advisor uses it. More trustworthy than a cheap NPK probe.

Optional: second physical domain

INA219 current sensor, LEDs or small fans, small battery. About NPR 1,000–1,500. Reuses the same ESP32s to run a power-budget demo.

Also needed

Your own Wi-Fi router for the exhibition, so nothing depends on venue internet. Software cost is zero: everything runs locally.

Software stack

Firmware

Arduino framework on ESP32, WiFi.h and PubSubClient for MQTT. Auto-reconnect built in.

Backend

Python, FastAPI, paho-mqtt, SQLite, WebSockets for live updates.

Prediction

Small ML models (ridge regression, gradient boosting, Holt-Winters trends) forecast drying rate, time to critical and reservoir depletion, with weather from Open-Meteo. They run in milliseconds with no GPU. Accuracy is shown as predicted vs actual, and any synthetic data is labeled.

Agents

LangGraph runs the irrigation, climate, crop and soil agents plus the arbiter. The language model (local through Ollama, or the Claude API) only explains decisions, handles Nepali and drafts advice. It never controls the pump.

Dashboard

Next.js as an installable web app (PWA). Works on phone and desktop with no Electron needed.

Knowledge base

Crop rules for Nepal: growth stages, ideal temperature and moisture ranges, planting seasons. The agents reason over this, so advice is grounded and checkable.

Simulator

Fake nodes publishing the same messages as real hardware, so development never waits on wiring.

Beyond irrigation

Irrigation is the demonstration, not the limit. The same engine loads a different scenario pack (a config file with entities, units, thresholds and labels) to run another domain with no core code changes.

Honest risks

  • No historical data. We will not claim deep learning on past data. The model learns online and any synthetic data is disclosed.
  • Sensor noise. Capacitive sensors drift. Calibration and smoothing are required.
  • LLMs are slow and unpredictable. So they never decide or control the pump. Small ML models predict, a deterministic allocator decides, and the safety layer guards.
  • “Why not just if-else?” Answer: if-else cannot optimally split limited water across competing demands with forecasts. The benchmark graph is the proof.
  • Reskin risk. Scenario packs must differ in real dynamics, not just labels.
  • Autonomy limits. Only water actions run on their own. Fertilizer, planting and other advice always waits for a human.
  • Nutrient claims. Without NPK sensors, nutrient advice is an estimate from rules and history, and is labeled that way.
  • Venue Wi-Fi. Bring our own router and keep everything local.

Bonus: satellite view

Only if the core is finished on time. It adds a view of a real field from space, at no cost, and feeds the same agent team.

Free data

Sentinel-2 (10 m detail, about every 5 days), Landsat, Google Earth Engine (free for non-commercial use) and NASA POWER for weather history.

What it adds

Vegetation (NDVI), moisture (NDMI) and chlorophyll (NDRE) maps. Flags parts of a field that look stressed and may need water or nutrients.

Limits

10 m pixels can’t see the table model, so it runs on a real nearby field. Monsoon clouds cause gaps. It shows possible stress, never an exact nutrient diagnosis, so advice says “verify with a soil test.”
